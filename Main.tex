\section{Introduction}

Ground-based astronomical spectra are affected by absorption in the Earth’s atmosphere. These telluric absorption features contaminate the observed spectrum and can interfere with the detection and analysis of astrophysical spectral lines, particularly when stellar and atmospheric features overlap in wavelength. Accurate modelling and correction of telluric absorption is therefore an essential step in the reduction and interpretation of many spectroscopic observations.

\section{PyMolFit framework}

PyMolFit is an independent pure-Python package for model-based telluric correction of astronomical spectra. The software combines physically based atmospheric modelling with radiative-transfer calculations and spectroscopic molecular databases to estimate the atmospheric transmission and produce a telluric-corrected estimate of the astronomical spectrum.

While several software packages for telluric correction are already available, PyMolFit was developed to provide an independent and extensible framework that integrates naturally into modern astronomical data-analysis workflows. Python is widely used for astronomical data reduction and analysis, with widely adopted libraries such as NumPy, SciPy, and Astropy forming the foundation of many reduction pipelines and analysis tools, implementing the complete telluric-correction workflow within the Python ecosystem enables direct integration into existing scientific software. The modular design of PyMolFit further facilitates validation, maintenance, and future development, allowing individual physical models and numerical components to be extended or replaced while preserving the overall framework.

\subsection{Relationship to Molecfit}
PyMolFit builds upon the physical methodology established by \Molecfit\ \citep{MOLECFIT1,MOLECFIT2}, adopting the same general approach to model-based telluric correction based on atmospheric modelling and line-by-line radiative-transfer calculations. Rather than interfacing with or executing Molecfit itself, PyMolFit provides an independent implementation of these methods written entirely in Python. The software therefore reproduces the overall modelling philosophy while allowing tighter integration with modern Python-based astronomical analysis workflows.

PyMolFit is not intended to be a bit-for-bit reproduction of either Molecfit or LBLRTM. It uses an independent numerical implementation, and remaining differences in the radiative-transfer and optimization procedures are evaluated in Sect.~\ref{sec:validation}.

PyMolFit exposes the telluric-correction workflow through a unified Python API that accepts spectrum files, loaded spectrum objects, or wavelength--flux arrays. The public workflow includes automatic segmentation while preserving physical detector or order groups, automatic and interactive fit-region selection, and optional treatment of theoretical stellar spectra through feature masking or a joint
stellar--telluric forward model. PyMolFit does not introduce a new atmospheric-spectroscopy formalism; instead, it implements established atmospheric and line-by-line radiative-transfer methods in Python without executing the Molecfit or LBLRTM software.

\subsection{Public workflow and software design}

PyMolFit provides a single entry point, \texttt{correct()}, for correcting spectrum files, loaded spectrum objects, or wavelength--flux arrays. All input routes use the same correction pipeline. PyMolFit handles tasks such as numerical segmentation, atmospheric-profile construction, fit-region processing, and model selection using metadata obtained from the input file or supplied explicitly by the user. The returned result contains the corrected spectrum, fitted atmospheric transmission, diagnostic information, and provenance. The automation is standard, although every parameter that is used for correction can be adjusted.

\section{Methodology}

\subsection{Overall workflow}

\subsection{Input spectrum and metadata}
\pymolfit PyMolFit first converts the input spectrum and its observational metadata into a standardised internal representation while preserving uncertainties, quality flags, and detector or spectral-order structure. It then constructs an atmospheric profile, calculates a high-resolution transmission spectrum, transforms this model to the observational domain, and fits it to the selected pixels. The fitted transmission is finally evaluated across the complete spectrum and used to produce the telluric-corrected output.

\subsection{Atmospheric modelling}
The observational metadata are subsequently used to construct a layered atmospheric profile above the observatory. PyMolFit combines a \MIPAS \citep{MIPAS} reference atmosphere with \GDAS \citep{GDAS} pressure, temperature and water-vapour data. When available, time-local \GDAS profiles are interpolated to the observation time; otherwise, an appropriate average profile is used. Surface meteorological measurements from the spectrum metadata are incorporated into the lowest atmospheric layers. The resulting profile provides the pressure, temperature, molecular composition and line-of-sight column density of each layer required by the radiative-transfer calculation.

\subsection{Molecular absorption and atmospheric continua}
Molecular absorption is calculated on a wavelength grid finer than the observed detector sampling. Relevant transitions are selected from the AER \citep{AER} molecular catalogue, and their temperature-dependent strengths, pressure shifts, Doppler widths and collision-broadened widths are evaluated independently in every atmospheric layer. The resulting Voigt profiles are multiplied by the corresponding molecular columns and summed over all lines and layers. This produces a species-resolved optical-depth model, from which the intrinsic high-resolution atmospheric transmission is obtained through the Beer–Lambert relation \(T(\lambda)=\exp[-\tau(\lambda)]\) \citep{RYBICKI1979}.

\subsection{Instrumental, wavelength, and continuum modelling}
Residual errors in the wavelength solution are represented by a wavelength-correction model. The transmission spectrum is then convolved with a parametrised line-spread function to account for the finite instrumental resolution and integrated onto the detector wavelength grid. A smooth polynomial continuum scales the resulting transmission model to the observed flux level.

\subsection{Forward model}
The forward model converts the high-resolution atmospheric transmission into a prediction of the observed flux. It combines the atmospheric transmission with a smooth representation of the source continuum and applies the wavelength and instrumental response. The resulting model has the same wavelength sampling as the input spectrum and can therefore be compared directly with the observed flux during fitting.


\subsection{Optimization}
The atmospheric and instrumental parameters are determined through bounded non-linear least-squares optimization. At each iteration, the species-resolved optical depths are scaled and combined, converted into transmission, wavelength corrected, convolved with the instrumental line-spread function, and re-binned onto the observed detector grid. A polynomial continuum converts the transmission into a model flux, which is compared with the observation over the selected fitting pixels. Molecular columns, wavelength coefficients and instrumental widths are iteratively updated until the residuals or parameter changes satisfy the convergence criteria.


\subsection{Outputs}
Following convergence, the fitting masks are removed and the optimized atmospheric, wavelength and instrumental parameters are applied over the complete spectral range. The high-resolution transmission is recomputed, convolved with the fitted line-spread function and re-binned onto every valid detector pixel. Fit and exclusion regions therefore affect parameter estimation only; atmospheric transmission is still evaluated within excluded regions and outside the intervals used for fitting. This produces the final detector-sampled transmission spectrum used for telluric correction.

The telluric-corrected spectrum is obtained by dividing the observed flux by the optimized detector-sampled atmospheric transmission. The original wavelength sampling, spectral-order structure and detector gaps are retained, while observational and model uncertainties are propagated through the division. Pixels with transmission below a specified threshold are masked because the atmospheric absorption has removed too much information for a stable correction. The final product includes the corrected flux together with the fitted transmission, model parameters, masks and provenance information.
